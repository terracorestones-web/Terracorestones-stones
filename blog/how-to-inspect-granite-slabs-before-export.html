How to Inspect Granite Slabs Before Export from India: A Buyer's Checklist

Kush Sharma · September 2026 · 8 min read

A beautiful photo is not an inspection. Between you and a container of mismatched, cracked, or thin slabs stands one thing: what you verify before it ships. This guide lays out what to check, in what order, and which numbers to hold your supplier to.

I've spent 15+ years inside India's stone trade, and the losses I've seen were almost never caused by dishonesty. They were caused by buyers approving material from a handful of close-up photos, with no agreed tolerance, no slab numbers, and no packing standard in the purchase order.

Key Takeaways
  • Inspect the full slab face under consistent light, not supplier close-ups. Ask for bundle-by-bundle photos with slab numbers you can trace to the packing list.
  • Hold floor slabs to 16-18 mm finished thickness and counters to 18-20 mm; measure multiple points yourself or demand caliper photos.
  • Run three field tests before approving: raking torch light for polish and pits, a wet cloth for hidden fills, and a coin-edge pass across suspect lines for cracks.
  • Black and galaxy granites get dyed. The acetone rub test and a face-vs-edge color comparison expose it in seconds.
  • Confirm tolerances in writing before production: around 1 mm on thickness and 2 mm on length and width is the industry norm for calibrated material.
  • Inspect packing while the container is open. After it's sealed, photos prove nothing about how slabs were separated or restrained.

Why Photo Approval Fails Buyers

Suppliers' photos show selected slabs from flattering angles under factory lighting. Yellow shop light hides flaws and exaggerates color. What a photo cannot show you: thickness variation across the slab, resin holding a slab together, dye on a tired black granite, or the difference between a natural fissure and a crack that will run once the slab is cut.

The fix is not more photos. It is a defined inspection protocol with acceptance criteria written into the purchase order before the slabs are processed. Natural stone varies by nature, so the goal of inspection is not zero variation. It is confirming the shipped material matches what you approved, with defects inside limits you agreed in advance.

The Inspection Checklist, Stage by Stage

1. Verify the Order and the Lot

Match the purchase order, approved finish, quantity, dimensions, tolerance, and packing instructions against what the factory presents. Confirm the stone's commercial name, the production lot, and the bundle allocation. Granite names vary between markets, so identify material by lot and slab number, not by color name alone.

2. Review the Full Slab Face

Each approved slab needs a readable number and a full-face image shot under stable lighting, so you can compare adjacent slabs and judge color movement across the lot. Close-ups are for repair detail, not for approving a shipment. For strongly veined material, review the numbered slab sequence before fabrication, especially if the project needs bookmatching.

3. Check Structural Condition

Walk the slab face and edges. You are looking for open cracks, unstable fissures, broken corners, heavy filling, and poor resin treatment. A fissure is a natural line following the stone's mineral structure; it does not open up in fabrication and is usually acceptable. A crack is a structural break; if you can feel it with a coin edge, it will run once the slab is cut, and the slab is a reject. Mark unacceptable areas with the slab number before approval.

4. Measure Dimensions the Right Way

Take thickness readings at multiple agreed positions and record individual results, not an average that hides a thin corner. Confirm usable length and width, not the rough overall size, and note damaged edges that reduce yield. For cut-to-size work, check squareness and compare finished pieces against the latest approved drawing.

The tolerance norms for calibrated granite: about 1 mm on thickness and 2 mm on length and width. Quoted thickness and delivered thickness are different things more often than buyers expect, which is why you measure or demand caliper photos.

5. Inspect the Finish

Polished granite should reflect clearly, like a mirror, with no dull patches, waves, swirl marks, or visible abrasive lines. Dull zones and uneven shine mean the polishing line was rushed or the slab was re-polished to hide repairs. Honed, flamed, leathered, and bush-hammered finishes each need their own approved reference sample, judged on texture evenness rather than gloss.

A gloss meter gives you numbers if the project needs them; your eye at a low angle catches what the meter misses.

6. Run the Three Field Tests

The raking light test. Hold a phone torch at a low angle across the surface. Raking light throws up scratches, pits, uneven polish, and filled areas you cannot see straight-on.

The wet cloth test. Wipe a section with a wet cloth. Wetting previews how the slab looks sealed and reveals patches and fills that disappear when dry.

The tap test. Stand the slab on edge and tap several spots. A clear ringing tone is sound stone. A dull, flat thud points to an internal crack or a slab held together by resin.

7. Catch Dyed and Resin-Flooded Granite

Cheap or aged black and galaxy granites are sometimes coated with a color-enhancing dye to fake depth. It fades and patches within a year or two of installation. Two checks expose it:

  • Face versus edge: look at the sawn back and cut edge. Natural granite is the same color through the stone. If the face is rich black and the edge is grey, the face is dyed.
  • The acetone rub: dab nail-polish remover on a hidden corner. Wipe with a white cloth. Natural granite leaves the cloth clean. Dyed granite transfers color.

A little factory resin on a few natural pits is normal. A slab reading dull on tap with a faint plasticky sheen in patches has been resin-flooded to hide micro-cracks. Reject it.

8. Confirm Quantity and Traceability

Reconcile accepted slabs against the packing list, bundle numbers, and shipping marks. Slab numbers, bundle numbers, and item codes must stay consistent across your inspection photos, the inspection report, and the final packing list. If accepted slabs cannot be matched to the shipment, your inspection evidence is worthless in a dispute.

9. Inspect Packing Before the Container Closes

Packing inspection only counts while supports, separators, and labels are still visible. Confirm strong wooden bundles or A-frames suited to the slab size, bottom support, plastic or foam separation between slabs, edge protection, moisture control for the destination route, and tight restraint against container movement. Record the loading sequence and take photos of the restraint before closure.

After the container is sealed, no photo can prove how slabs were separated or whether they can shift in transit.

Gangsaw or Cutter: It Changes What You Inspect

Gangsaw slabs are cut from large blocks on multi-blade machines into big, uniform, large-format slabs, typically over 9 feet long. These are the premium, even slabs you want for floors and counters. Cutter slabs come off smaller single-blade machines: shorter, often slightly uneven, fine for sills, treads, and small areas, but they mean more joints and more variation across a floor. Confirm which you are buying in writing, because the price gap is where substitutions happen. Our granite sourcing overview covers the grades and finishes to specify.

The Export Document Checklist

Inspection confirms the material. Documents prove it legally crossed borders. Before you pay the balance, confirm:

  • Proforma and commercial invoice
  • Packing list with crate count, dimensions, net and gross weight, slabs per crate, and bundle marks
  • Bill of lading
  • Certificate of origin
  • Insurance certificate
  • Test report or quality certificate, including water absorption (under 0.5% for dense premium granite) and density around 2.63-2.75 g/cm³
  • The inspection report itself, with photographs traceable to slab and bundle numbers

Remote Inspection: Your Three Options

Most buyers cannot stand in the factory in Rajasthan or Andhra Pradesh. You have three workable routes:

  1. A defined photo and video protocol. Bundle-by-bundle full-slab photos under consistent light, numbered slabs, caliper readings in frame, and a container loading video. Cheap, and enough for repeat orders with a proven supplier.
  2. Live video inspection during production and at loading, walking the bundles with the factory's QA team on camera.
  3. An independent on-ground inspector for first orders, premium material, or strict projects: someone physically present at the factory during your production run, at packing, and at container sign-off. International agencies like SGS, TÜV SÜD, and Bureau Veritas operate in India; specialist stone inspection firms work the quarry belts directly.

The right choice tracks the risk. A first container of premium black granite deserves eyes on the ground. A repeat order of the same material from the same lot does not.

Red Flags That Predict a Bad Container

  • No full-slab photos, only close-up beauty shots
  • Generic color names with no current stock confirmation or lot number
  • No written position on cracks, resin, repairs, or acceptable variation
  • No packaging specification for export shipment
  • No loading photos or inspection access
  • Price that changes after the deposit, with a quiet change in grade, thickness, or finish

Any two of these together, ask for inspection before releasing the balance payment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I inspect granite slabs only by photos?

Photo inspection works for early approval and repeat orders when the protocol is strict: full-slab numbered photos under consistent light, caliper readings, and loading video. For first orders, premium material, or projects with strict color and fabrication requirements, put a person at the factory or on video at loading.

What are the most common defects found at pre-shipment inspection?

Color mismatch between bundles, unstable cracks, inconsistent finish, thickness below spec, and weak packing. All five are cheap to catch at the factory and expensive to discover at your port.

Should I inspect before or after packing?

Both stages matter. Slab quality is checked before packing, while faces and edges are visible. Packing quality is checked after crating, before the container closes, when separators and restraint can still be corrected.

What should a slab inspection report contain?

The order and material identity, lot and bundle numbers, quantity, individual dimension readings, the approved appearance range, defects and repairs by slab number, packing status, photographs linked to slab numbers, and the final disposition of any rejected slabs.

How do I judge color consistency without seeing the slabs in person?

Compare numbered full-face photos taken under the same light, slab to slab and bundle to bundle, against the approved reference. Ask for the same lot, and reject mixing of lots within one bundle. Wet a corner on video to preview the sealed color.

Conclusion

Granite inspection is not about eliminating natural variation. It is about making the shipped material match what you approved, with numbers and photos that survive a dispute. Approve the color range before production. Number your slabs. Measure instead of trusting. Test for dye and resin on anything black. And never let a container close without seeing how it was packed.

The buyers who lose money in India are not the ones who inspect too much. They are the ones who approved a shipment from five close-up photos and a WhatsApp message.
